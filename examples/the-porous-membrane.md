# The Porous Membrane: Communities That Absorb Their Deviants

> **A sample trail generated by the Syntopic Reading System.**
> A "trail" is a sequence of excerpts drawn from *different* books, connected by a single question -

**Description:** How certain social ecosystems metabolize eccentricity into civic identity rather
than treating it as a pathogen - from Savannah's tolerance of Jim Williams to Dickens's London
parishes to the shtetl logic of sheltering holy fools.

**Rationale:** By tracing how *Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil*, *Bleak House*, and *The
Brothers Karamazov* each depict communities with radically different thresholds for absorbing
strangeness, we can map the structural conditions - porosity, patronage, shared myth - that
determine whether a community load-bears or expels its outliers.

## Trail

### 1 · *The 48 Laws of Power* - Robert Greene
**Setting:** Robert Greene recounts ancient Athenian politics: how figures like Damon and Hyperbolus
were driven out via ostracism, while Pericles survived by staying connected to ordinary people.
**Connection:** Athens represents the impermeable membrane - a community that systematically expelled
its deviants rather than metabolizing them. Yet the system collapsed when applied to a mere buffoon
instead of a formidable figure, suggesting the expulsion mechanism depends on the deviant's stature -
and that communities lose something essential when they cannot distinguish dangerous dissent from
productive eccentricity.

> *In 417 B.C., Hyperbolus saw an opportunity to stir up anger against the two leading politicians of
> the time… But Alcibiades and Nicias, although they were otherwise enemies, pooled their resources
> and managed to turn the ostracism on Hyperbolus instead. **His obnoxiousness, they argued, could
> only be terminated by banishment.***
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 2 · *The Righteous Mind* - Jonathan Haidt
**Setting:** Haidt uses the Chinese concept of yin and yang, plus quotes from John Stuart Mill and
Bertrand Russell, to argue that liberal and conservative temperaments function as complementary
opposites within a healthy society.
**Connection:** Haidt's Durkheimian framework provides the theoretical spine for the trail:
communities that absorb their deviants navigate between ossification and dissolution, maintaining the
porous membrane that lets eccentricity circulate as moral capital rather than calcifying into rigid
orthodoxy or dissolving into atomized individualism.

> *Haidt, quoting Bertrand Russell:* "**Every community is exposed to two opposite dangers:
> ossification through too much discipline and reverence for tradition, on the one hand; on the other
> hand, dissolution… through the growth of an individualism and personal independence that makes
> cooperation impossible.**"
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 3 · *The Scarlet Letter* - Nathaniel Hawthorne  *(public domain)*
**Setting:** In Puritan Boston, Hester Prynne and her daughter Pearl await a public procession in the
marketplace. Pearl asks about the old jailer who has just nodded at them, and about "the minister"
(Dimmesdale, her secret father).
**Connection:** Pearl's sharp-eyed observation that the jailer nods familiarly at her branded mother
reveals how Puritan Boston kept Hester inside its membrane even while marking her as deviant. The
scarlet letter is not exile but absorption - the community metabolizes her sin into civic spectacle,
compressing its own need for transgression into the body of the woman it refuses to fully expel.

> "And there is Master Brackett, the old jailer, nodding and smiling at me. Why does he do so,
> mother?" "He remembers thee a little babe, my child," answered Hester. "He should not nod and smile
> at me, for all that,—the black, grim, ugly-eyed old man!" said Pearl. "He may nod at thee, if he
> will; for thou art clad in gray, and wearest the scarlet letter. But see, mother, how many faces of
> strange people, and Indians among them, and sailors!… They wait to see the procession pass," said
> Hester. "For the Governor and the magistrates are to go by, and the ministers, and all the great
> people and good people, with the music and the soldiers marching before them." "And will the
> minister be there?" asked Pearl… "He will be there, child," answered her mother. "But he will not
> greet thee to-day; nor must thou greet him." "What a strange, sad man is he!" said the child… "In
> the dark night-time he calls us to him… But here, in the sunny day, and among all the people, he
> knows us not; nor must we know him!"

### 4 · *Jane Eyre* - Charlotte Brontë  *(public domain)*
**Setting:** Jane has reconciled with Hannah, a servant in the Rivers household, who chats while they
bake, sharing local gossip and family history about the Rivers and Oliver families.
**Connection:** Hannah's dialect-rich acceptance of the destitute stranger enacts the porous parish
membrane at its most intimate: class, education, and origin dissolve in the kitchen's flour-dusted
handshake. The Rivers family, themselves "of a mak' of their own," have already taught this household
that eccentricity is simply how belonging sounds in a particular accent.

> "**You look a raight down dacent little crater.**" "That will do — I forgive you now. Shake hands."
> She put her floury and horny hand into mine; another and heartier smile illumined her rough face,
> and from that moment we were friends… Old Mr. Rivers, she said, was a plain man enough, but a
> gentleman, and of as ancient a family as could be found. Marsh End had belonged to the Rivers ever
> since it was a house… The mistress was different. She was a great reader, and studied a deal; and
> the "bairns" had taken after her. There was nothing like them in these parts, nor ever had been…
> they had always been "of a mak' of their own."

### 5 · *Secondhand Time* - Svetlana Alexievich  *(English translation)*
**Setting:** One interviewee's memory of growing up in a Soviet communal children's institution,
describing the enforced togetherness, shared knowledge of each other's private habits, and strict
nightly routines.
**Connection:** The Soviet orphanage is the porous membrane pushed to its most extreme and savage
form: a community so radically proximate that every vulnerability is shared knowledge, every
deviance witnessed and remembered. The children metabolize each other's strangeness not through
tolerance but through the sheer impossibility of privacy - turning collective exposure into the only
identity available.

> "We all knew each other in intimate detail: who wet their bed, who screamed in their sleep, who
> couldn't pronounce their r's… There were forty metal beds in one room… In the evening, they'd order
> us: 'Palms together, hands under your cheeks, and everyone on your right side.' And we were supposed
> to do all that all together. Everyone! **That was community — maybe it was savage… but that's how I
> was brought up.**"
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 6 · *When* - Daniel H. Pink
**Setting:** Pink introduces Mumbai's dabbawalas - a low-tech network of lunchbox deliverymen with
minimal formal education - as a striking example of a highly reliable logistical system that seems
implausible on paper.
**Connection:** The dabbawalas invert the usual logic of deviance and exclusion: illiteracy and lack
of formal education, which would mark these men as deficient in most institutional settings, become
irrelevant inside a community whose membrane is built on belonging, touch, and shared codes rather
than credentialed hierarchy. The group absorbs what the world calls deficiency and converts it into
astonishing precision.

> "**Most of them have, at best, an eighth grade education. Many of them cannot read or write, a fact
> that only deepens the implausibility of what they do.**"… the dabbawalas' error rate is claimed at
> 1 in 16 million. "…they must synch to the tribe — to one another. That requires a deep sense of
> belonging."
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 7 · *The Square and the Tower* - Niall Ferguson
**Setting:** Ferguson notes that the word "network" is historically recent (absent even from an 1869
Trollope novel), then introduces the concept of hierarchy using a Venetian church mosaic of the Last
Judgement as a visual metaphor.
**Connection:** Ferguson's distinction between hierarchies and informal networks maps directly onto
the trail's central question: rigid towers expel what they cannot classify, while the square - the
porous, networked space of reciprocal exchange - creates niches where eccentrics and outsiders find
roles through mutual accommodation rather than top-down assignment.

> "The word 'network' does not appear once in [Trollope's 1869 *Phineas Finn*]… Inside the
> Cattedrale di Santa Maria Assunta is a perfect illustration of what we mean by hierarchy: an
> eleventh-century mosaic of the Last Judgement in five tiers, **with Christ at the top and the fires
> of hell at the bottom.**"
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 8 · *The Effingers* - Gabriele Tergit  *(English translation)*
**Setting:** Family members argue in real time about whether to emigrate or stay in Berlin as
conditions worsen under the Nazi regime - debating money, safety, and moral principle.
**Connection:** *The Effingers* reveals the lethal inversion of the porous membrane: a community that
once absorbed a Jewish family so thoroughly that its name became synonymous with German industrial
identity now hardens into a regime that leaves "no cracks left to slip through." When a membrane
calcifies, it devours the very eccentricity that gave it life.

> "Since the dawn of time, refugees have tried to take what they could with them. But **this
> persecution is so efficiently organized there are no cracks left to slip through.**"
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 9 · *Atlas Shrugged* - Ayn Rand
**Setting:** Mr. Mowen, an older factory owner, reacts with bewildered distress as he watches a
familiar industrial neighbor's business shut down and disappear, lamenting to a young bystander how
the world has changed.
**Connection:** Mr. Mowen's bewilderment exposes what happens when a community's membrane was so
porous it absorbed its industries as organs of collective selfhood: the departure is experienced not
as a business event but as an amputation. Read against the trail, it reveals the psychic cost when a
community that metabolized its members' enterprises suddenly discovers the membrane was permeable in
*both* directions.

> "It isn't right," he said… "A man is part of his community, he's got a right to count on it,
> hasn't he?… I was born here. I expected the old companies to be here when I grew up… **Something
> ought to be done about it.**" "About what?"
> - quoted briefly under fair use

### 10 · *A Study in Scarlet* - Arthur Conan Doyle  *(public domain)*
**Setting:** In the novel's flashback to the American West, John Ferrier and a young girl, both near
death in the desert, are discovered and rescued by a large Mormon wagon train.
**Connection:** Ferrier's defiant adoption of Lucy - "I guess she is now" - mirrors the Mormon
community's own logic of absorption: a persecuted people extend their membrane to encompass two
strangers precisely because shared extremity dissolves the distinction between insider and deviant.
The community forged by exile becomes maximally porous at its margins, taking in what it finds at the
threshold of survival.

> The man staggered to his feet… "This is what they call delirium, I guess," he muttered… "My name is
> John Ferrier," the wanderer explained; "me and that little un are all that's left o' twenty-one
> people…" "Is she your child?" asked someone. "**I guess she is now,**" the other cried, defiantly;
> "she's mine 'cause I saved her. No man will take her from me. She's Lucy Ferrier from this day on…"
> "…we are the persecuted children of God — the chosen of the Angel Merona."

## The synthesis

*The tool surfaces the trail - the excerpts and their connections - and drafts a rough synthesis to
argue with. The finished synthesis is the payoff you build by refining that draft with your own
judgment. Here is where a reader took this one:*

Read as one, the ten converge on a single mechanism with two directions. A community's membrane is
only as valuable as it is porous: the same permeability that lets it take in a branded adulteress, a
destitute stranger in a parish kitchen, an illiterate courier, or a child at the threshold of death is
what keeps it adaptive and alive. Athens shows the cost of the impermeable membrane - it expels until
nothing is left to expel but buffoons, and the mechanism itself degrades. Haidt names the law
underneath: ossify and you die, dissolve and you die; survival lives in the tension between them. And
*The Effingers* and *Atlas Shrugged* mark the two failure modes at the edges - a membrane that
calcifies into a regime with "no cracks left to slip through," and one so total that when a member
leaves, the loss registers as an amputation.

The porous membrane, then, is not tolerance as a virtue. It is tolerance as a load-bearing structure.
The communities that can metabolize their strange ones are the ones supple enough to survive their own
future - because the deviant they shelter today is the adaptation they will need tomorrow. That is the
argument none of these ten authors wrote, and all of them, read together, make.
